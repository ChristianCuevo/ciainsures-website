Negligent Security Liability: Lessons from Cox v. Keg Restaurant for Security Companies

As demand for security personnel continues to grow, so does the legal exposure faced by businesses, property owners, and security providers. One of the most common sources of liability in the industry is negligent security—claims that a business failed to provide reasonable protection against foreseeable criminal acts.

Understanding how courts evaluate these cases is critical for security companies and the clients they serve. The case of Cox v. Keg Restaurant (Washington Court of Appeals, 1997) offers important insight into when a business may—or may not—be held responsible after a violent incident.

What Is Negligent Security?

Negligent security is a type of premises liability claim. It arises when a property owner or operator fails to take reasonable steps to prevent foreseeable harm caused by criminal activity.

Common negligent security claims involve:

  • Assault and battery (approximately 42%)
  • Sexual assault (26%)
  • Wrongful death (15%)
  • Robbery (9%)
  • False imprisonment and other incidents (8%)

Courts typically evaluate three key questions:

  1. Was the risk foreseeable?
  2. Were reasonable security measures in place?
  3. Did a failure in security directly contribute to the injury?

For security companies, these cases often translate into allegations of inadequate staffing, poor response procedures, or failure to follow established protocols.

Case Summary: Cox v. Keg Restaurant

In the Cox case, a bar manager was warned that an intoxicated patron, Philip Whalen, posed a threat to others. The manager removed Whalen from the premises. However, Whalen later re-entered the bar and, after some time, violently attacked another patron, Cox, causing severe brain injuries.

Cox filed a lawsuit claiming the restaurant was negligent for failing to prevent the attacker from returning.

Court Decision

The Washington Court of Appeals ruled in favor of the restaurant, finding that:

  • The business had taken reasonable action by removing the individual.
  • The subsequent attack was not sufficiently foreseeable to impose liability.
  • The restaurant’s actions met the standard of reasonable care under the circumstances.

This case illustrates an important principle: businesses are not automatically liable for every criminal act that occurs on their property—only when they fail to take reasonable precautions against foreseeable risks.

Risk Lessons for Security Companies

While the defendant prevailed, the case highlights several operational risks that frequently appear in negligent security claims.

1. Foreseeability Is Everything

If a threat is identified—such as a disruptive or aggressive individual—documentation and follow-through are critical. Failure to act consistently can create liability.

2. Access Control Matters

If an individual is removed from a property, procedures should address:

  • Monitoring exits and re-entry points
  • Communication between staff
  • Incident tracking

3. Incident Reporting Protects You

Detailed incident reports help demonstrate that appropriate action was taken and are often a key defense in litigation.

4. Training and Procedures Reduce Exposure

Courts look for evidence of:

  • Written security policies
  • Staff training
  • Proper supervision
  • Adequate staffing levels

Why Insurance Is Critical in Negligent Security Cases

Even when a business or security provider ultimately wins a case, legal defense costs alone can be substantial. Security companies should ensure their insurance program addresses the unique risks of the industry, including:

  • General liability (assault and battery coverage)
  • Professional liability / errors and omissions
  • Workers’ compensation
  • Umbrella / excess liability
  • Contractual liability requirements

Many negligent security claims arise from contract disputes or client requirements, making proper coverage essential for both protection and contract compliance.

Protecting Your Security Business

Negligent security cases like Cox v. Keg Restaurant demonstrate that liability often depends on whether reasonable steps were taken—not whether an incident occurred.

Security companies can reduce risk by:

  • Implementing strong incident reporting procedures
  • Training guards on escalation and removal protocols
  • Maintaining clear post orders and documentation
  • Reviewing contracts and insurance requirements regularly
