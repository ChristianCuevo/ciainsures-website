Illinois Security Company Licensing and Insurance Considerations

Insurance and licensing needs can vary by service type, employee duties, firearms exposure, alarm work, local requirements, and client contracts. Therefore, Illinois security businesses should review their licenses, contracts, and insurance policies together.

Private Security Contractor and Agency Licensing in Illinois

Illinois regulates private security contractors and private security contractor agencies through the Illinois Department of Financial and Professional Regulation. IDFPR lists security professions that include Licensed Private Security Contractor, Licensed Private Security Contractor Agency, agency branch office, Firearm Control Card, Permanent Employee Registration Card, approved 20-hour basic training program, and approved basic 48-hour firearm training course.

Illinois law also states that a person cannot act as a private security contractor, private alarm contractor, fingerprint vendor, locksmith, or private detective, or advertise as one, unless licensed by the Department. In addition, a firm, corporation, or other legal entity cannot act as a licensed agency in those categories unless IDFPR licenses it.

Because of that, security company insurance should support both your business operations and your Illinois licensing or registration needs.

Illinois Private Security Contractor Insurance Minimum

IDFPR’s Private Security Contractor qualifications state that applicants must provide proof of coverage under a commercial general liability insurance policy with at least $1,000,000 of liability insurance.

However, that minimum may not satisfy every contract. For example, a hospital, school, warehouse, municipality, large event venue, retail center, construction site, or corporate client may require higher limits, umbrella coverage, waiver of subrogation, primary and noncontributory wording, or additional insured status.

Also, armed security companies need to review firearm-related wording carefully. IDFPR’s private security contractor insurance form states that the commercial general liability policy must include bodily injury liability, property damage, and personal injury coverage. In addition, if the licensee carries a firearm in the course of duty, or if a licensee-in-charge permits agency employees to carry firearms, coverage must extend to claims involving injury or damage from firearm use while acting in the course of employment.

Private Security Contractor Agency Requirements

An Illinois private security contractor agency needs a full-time Illinois licensed private security licensee-in-charge. Also, Illinois law says no private security contractor may serve as licensee-in-charge for more than one private security contractor agency.

As a result, insurance reviews should confirm the correct named insured, locations, operations, employee duties, and firearm exposure. If your agency adds locations, employees, new services, or armed posts, review your policy before the next certificate request.

PERC Cards and Security Employee Requirements

Illinois security businesses should also review Permanent Employee Registration Card requirements. IDFPR’s PERC application states that applicants must be at least 18 years old to apply for a PERC in an unarmed capacity and must submit fingerprint background check information. The same IDFPR material notes that individuals with a valid Illinois detective, security contractor, alarm contractor, or locksmith license do not need a PERC to work for a licensed agency.

In addition, Illinois law requires registered employees of a private security contractor agency who provide traditional guarding, other private security functions, or alarm response to complete at least 20 hours of basic training within 30 days of employment. It also requires additional training within 6 months and annual refresher training after the first employment anniversary.

Because staffing, training, and employee duties affect risk, your insurance program should match how your employees work in the field.

Private Alarm Contractor and Security System Company Considerations

Some security companies also sell, install, service, operate, or monitor alarm systems. In that case, Illinois private alarm contractor rules may matter.

IDFPR lists private alarm professions that include Licensed Private Alarm Contractor, Licensed Private Alarm Contractor Agency, agency branch office, Firearm Control Card, PERC, approved 20-hour basic training, and approved basic 48-hour firearm training course.

Also, IDFPR’s Private Alarm Contractor qualifications state that applicants must provide proof of commercial general liability insurance with at least $1,000,000 of liability insurance. The same qualification document discusses experience involving entities that inspect, review, design, sell, install, operate, service, or monitor alarm systems.

Therefore, if your company handles alarms, CCTV, access control, monitoring, or alarm response, your policy review should confirm that your insurance accurately lists those operations.

Workers’ Compensation Insurance in Illinois

If your security company has employees, review workers’ compensation immediately. The Illinois Department of Insurance states that an employer with one employee, even a part-time employee, must provide workers’ compensation insurance coverage. It also explains that noncompliant employers may face fines, work-stop orders, and civil exposure.

This matters for security companies because employees may work nights, drive patrol routes, stand for long shifts, respond to incidents, confront trespassers, walk parking lots, use stairs, or work in high-risk locations.

As a result, your workers’ compensation policy should match your payroll, job duties, employee classifications, armed or unarmed operations, and Illinois work locations.

Types of Security Company Insurance We Can Review and Quote

Every security company operates differently. A small unarmed guard company may need different coverage than an armed patrol company, alarm response business, CCTV installer, event security firm, or access control contractor.

General Liability Insurance

General liability insurance often forms the foundation of a security company insurance program. A commercial general liability policy can help protect a business from financial loss when it faces liability for property damage, bodily injury, or personal and advertising injury caused by its services, operations, or employees.

For an Illinois security company, general liability may help with claims involving:

  • A visitor injury at a guarded property
  • Property damage caused during security operations
  • A client claim involving guard activity
  • Certain personal injury or advertising injury allegations
  • Certificate requirements from clients or property managers

However, security companies should not stop at basic general liability. Instead, review exclusions and endorsements carefully. Many security businesses need coverage that addresses assault and battery exposure, firearm liability, professional services, alarm response, mobile patrol, and completed operations.

Professional Liability / Errors and Omissions Insurance

Security companies can face claims that do not involve simple bodily injury or property damage. For example, a client may claim your company failed to prevent a theft, failed to respond properly, missed an incident during patrol, wrote an inaccurate report, or failed to follow post orders.

Therefore, professional liability or errors and omissions coverage can matter for:

  • Security consulting
  • Risk assessments
  • Security plans
  • Alarm response
  • Monitoring operations
  • Post-order failures
  • Incident reporting
  • Access control management
  • CCTV or alarm system recommendations

Because general liability may not cover every failure-to-perform claim, review E&O coverage before you sign larger contracts.

Assault and Battery Coverage

Security companies face a higher confrontation risk than many other businesses. A guard may break up a fight, escort someone off a property, intervene during a theft, respond to a disturbance, or use reasonable force during an incident.

However, some policies limit or exclude assault and battery claims. Therefore, Illinois security companies should review whether the policy addresses assault, battery, use of force, negligent hiring, negligent supervision, and related allegations.

This matters even for unarmed companies. If your guards interact with the public, patrol public-facing locations, or handle disputes, review this coverage carefully.

Armed Security and Firearm Liability Coverage

Armed security creates a higher risk profile. Because of that, underwriters often ask detailed questions about firearms, training, contracts, post orders, supervision, claims history, and client locations.

If your company provides armed guards, review whether your liability policy includes firearm-related claims while employees act within the scope of employment. Also, confirm whether your policy matches IDFPR insurance wording, client contracts, and any additional insured requirements.

Before you add armed services, request a policy review.

Workers’ Compensation Insurance

Workers’ compensation can help cover medical expenses and lost wages for covered employees injured on the job. For security companies, injuries may involve falls, assaults, long shifts, patrol duties, vehicle accidents, stairs, parking lots, crowd control, or emergency response.

A policy review can help verify:

  • Correct classification codes
  • Accurate payroll estimates
  • Armed vs. unarmed employee duties
  • Part-time and full-time payroll
  • Executive officer status
  • Subcontractor handling
  • Multi-state exposure
  • Audit preparation

Commercial Auto Insurance

Security companies often use patrol cars, marked vehicles, unmarked vehicles, supervisor vehicles, or company cars. Also, employees may drive between client sites, patrol lots, respond to alarms, or transport equipment.

Personal auto insurance usually does not fit business vehicle exposure. Therefore, commercial auto insurance deserves a careful review.

Commercial auto can help protect your business if a covered work vehicle accident occurs while your team drives for company operations. In addition, many contracts require specific auto liability limits.

Hired and Non-Owned Auto Coverage

Some security companies rely on employee-owned vehicles for patrol, site visits, alarm response, supervisor checks, or errands. However, that creates risk for the business if a claim names the company after an accident.

Hired and non-owned auto coverage can help protect the business when employees use vehicles the company does not own for business purposes.

Umbrella or Excess Liability Insurance

Many security contracts require higher limits than a standard policy provides. For example, a commercial property owner, school, hospital, construction site, apartment complex, or event venue may request $2 million, $5 million, or higher liability limits.

Umbrella or excess liability insurance can provide additional limits over qualifying underlying policies, such as general liability, commercial auto, and employers’ liability.

Also, umbrella coverage can help your company compete for larger contracts. Therefore, review your limits before you bid higher-value work.

Crime, Employee Dishonesty, and Third-Party Theft Coverage

Security employees may access keys, offices, storage rooms, equipment areas, vehicles, gates, cameras, access control systems, tenant areas, or restricted spaces. Because of that, clients may ask whether your business has crime or employee dishonesty coverage.

This coverage can help address certain theft-related risks. In addition, it may help satisfy contract requirements for clients who want protection against employee dishonesty.

Cyber Liability Insurance

Security companies may store customer records, incident reports, surveillance information, access credentials, schedules, employee data, client location information, or alarm system details.

Also, alarm, CCTV, access control, and monitoring companies often use cloud platforms, apps, remote access, portals, and connected devices. As a result, cyber liability should be reviewed.

Cyber coverage may help with certain costs tied to data breaches, cyber incidents, privacy claims, ransomware, and related response expenses.

Tools, Equipment, and Business Property Coverage

Security companies often rely on radios, uniforms, cameras, body cameras, office equipment, computers, mobile devices, access equipment, signage, patrol equipment, and other business property.

If your equipment travels between client locations, standard property coverage may not be enough. Therefore, tools and equipment coverage, often written as inland marine insurance, may help protect mobile property away from your main office.

Employment Practices Liability Insurance

Security companies often manage shift schedules, hiring, discipline, post assignments, uniforms, training, and terminations. As a result, employment-related claims can become a serious risk.

Employment practices liability insurance, often called EPLI, can help protect against certain covered claims involving discrimination, harassment, retaliation, wrongful termination, and other employment allegations.

Bonds

Some clients may require bonds before a security company can start work. Also, certain contracts may require fidelity bonds, employee dishonesty protection, or other bond-related documentation.

A bond does not work the same way as insurance. However, it can still support contract requirements and client trust.

FAQ: Security Company Insurance in Illinois

What insurance does a security company need in Illinois?

A security company in Illinois commonly needs general liability insurance, workers’ compensation if it has employees, professional liability or errors and omissions coverage, commercial auto insurance, and umbrella or excess liability. In addition, many security businesses should review assault and battery coverage, firearm liability, hired and non-owned auto, cyber liability, crime coverage, employee dishonesty coverage, EPLI, equipment coverage, and bonds.

Is security company insurance required in Illinois?

Illinois requires certain licensed security professionals to maintain insurance. IDFPR’s Private Security Contractor qualifications require proof of commercial general liability insurance with at least $1,000,000 of liability insurance. Also, Illinois employers with one employee, even a part-time employee, must provide workers’ compensation coverage unless an exception applies.

Does Illinois require a private security contractor license?

Yes. Illinois law states that a person may not act as or provide the functions of a private security contractor, private alarm contractor, fingerprint vendor, locksmith, or private detective, or advertise as one, unless licensed by IDFPR. It also requires firms, corporations, and other legal entities acting as licensed agencies to hold the proper agency license.

How much liability insurance does an Illinois private security contractor need?

IDFPR states that Private Security Contractor applicants must provide proof of a commercial general liability policy with at least $1,000,000 of liability insurance. However, client contracts may require higher limits, umbrella coverage, additional insured status, waiver of subrogation, or primary and noncontributory wording.

Do armed security companies in Illinois need special insurance?

Armed security companies should review firearm liability wording carefully. IDFPR’s private security contractor insurance form states that if the licensee carries a firearm in the course of duty, or if the licensee-in-charge permits agency employees to carry firearms, the policy must extend to claims involving injury or damage from firearm use while acting in the course of employment.

Do Illinois security guards need a PERC card?

Many security employees need a Permanent Employee Registration Card, commonly called a PERC. IDFPR’s PERC application states that applicants must be at least 18 years old for an unarmed capacity and must submit fingerprint background check information. Certain valid Illinois license holders, such as security contractors or alarm contractors, do not need a PERC to work for a licensed agency.

What training do Illinois private security employees need?

Illinois law requires registered employees of a private security contractor agency who provide traditional guarding, other private security functions, or alarm response to complete at least 20 hours of basic training within 30 days of employment. The law also includes additional training and annual refresher training requirements.

Do private alarm contractors in Illinois need insurance?

Yes. IDFPR’s Private Alarm Contractor qualifications require applicants to provide proof of commercial general liability insurance with at least $1,000,000 of liability insurance. Private alarm operations may include inspecting, reviewing, designing, selling, installing, operating, servicing, or monitoring alarm systems.

Does general liability cover assault and battery claims?

Not always. Security companies should review policy wording carefully because some policies limit or exclude assault and battery claims. Because guards may respond to confrontations, remove trespassers, or intervene during incidents, this coverage deserves special attention.

Does general liability cover failure to prevent theft?

Not always. A claim alleging that a security company failed to prevent theft, failed to respond properly, missed a patrol, or failed to follow post orders may require professional liability or errors and omissions coverage. Therefore, security companies should review both general liability and professional liability.

Do security companies need commercial auto insurance?

A security company should review commercial auto insurance if it owns patrol vehicles, supervisor vehicles, marked cars, unmarked cars, or other work vehicles. Also, if employees use personal vehicles for patrol, alarm response, errands, or site visits, hired and non-owned auto coverage may matter.
