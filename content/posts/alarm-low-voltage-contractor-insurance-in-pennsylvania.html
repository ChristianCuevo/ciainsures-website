Pennsylvania Insurance and Registration Considerations for Alarm & Security Contractors

Pennsylvania insurance requirements can vary depending on whether you work on residential properties, commercial properties, public projects, fire alarm systems, or municipal jobs.

Residential Security System Work and PA Home Improvement Registration

Pennsylvania’s Home Improvement Consumer Protection Act applies to many contractors who offer or perform home improvements in Pennsylvania. The Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General states that the law created a mandatory registration program for contractors who offer or perform home improvements, and its definition of home improvement includes security systems when performed in connection with certain private residences.

The same PA Attorney General FAQ states that anyone who owns or operates a home improvement business, or offers or performs home improvements in Pennsylvania, must register unless they fall within listed exceptions, such as small contractors performing less than $5,000 of home improvement work in a calendar year. The FAQ also states that locksmiths, burglar alarm businesses, fire alarm businesses, and similar businesses must register if they perform covered home improvement work on private residences.

For registration, the PA Attorney General FAQ says contractors must provide insurance policy information showing at least $50,000 of personal injury liability coverage and $50,000 of property damage coverage.

Commercial Low Voltage Work

The PA Attorney General FAQ states that HICPA applies to private residences and does not apply to home improvements done on commercial properties. However, commercial clients, landlords, property managers, general contractors, school districts, municipalities, healthcare facilities, and retail chains may still require their own insurance limits, endorsements, additional insured wording, waiver of subrogation, or certificates of insurance before work starts.

Local Licensing and Permit Rules

Pennsylvania does not have a universal statewide contractor license for every trade. The Pennsylvania Department of Labor & Industry notes that some municipalities have local licensure or certification requirements for contractors and construction trades, and that these commonly involve home improvement contractors, electrical contractors, electricians, plumbing contractors, and similar trades.

This matters for alarm, CCTV, access control, and fire alarm contractors because local requirements may change depending on where the work is performed.

For example, Philadelphia requires a Fire Alarm Inspector’s License to inspect, test, or certify fire alarm systems, unless the person is a licensed electrical contractor. The City also states that each inspector at a company must have their own license.

Philadelphia also lists insurance minimums for PA Home Improvement Contractors seeking construction permits, including general liability of $500,000 per occurrence, automobile liability insurance of $300,000, and workers’ compensation limits of $100,000 per accident, $100,000 per employee, and $500,000 policy limit.

Workers’ Compensation in Pennsylvania

If your alarm or low-voltage business has employees, workers’ compensation should be reviewed immediately. Pennsylvania’s Department of Labor & Industry states that workers’ compensation insurance is generally mandatory for employers with one or more employees, whether part-time or full-time, including family members.

Pennsylvania also states that the requirement to insure workers’ compensation liability is mandatory for any employer with at least one employee who could be injured or develop a work-related disease in Pennsylvania, unless all employees are excluded under the law.

Coverage Options for Alarm, CCTV, Access Control, and Low Voltage Contractors

A strong insurance program should match your actual operations. A company that only installs residential cameras may need a different setup than a fire alarm contractor, security integrator, access control company, or structured cabling business working in commercial buildings.

General Liability Insurance

General liability insurance is often the foundation of a contractor insurance program. It can help protect your business from third-party bodily injury, third-party property damage, and certain personal or advertising injury claims. The Insurance Information Institute describes commercial general liability as coverage that protects a business from financial loss when it is liable for property damage, bodily injury, or personal and advertising injury caused by business operations, services, or employees.

For alarm and low-voltage contractors, general liability may respond to claims such as damaging a customer’s wall during installation, a customer tripping over your tools, or accidental property damage during a service call.

Professional Liability / Errors & Omissions Coverage

General liability may not be enough for alarm, CCTV, fire alarm, and access control businesses. A customer may not only claim that you caused physical damage. They may claim that your service, advice, installation design, system configuration, inspection, or maintenance failed to perform as expected.

Professional liability, also called errors and omissions coverage, can be important for claims involving alleged mistakes, negligence, or failure to deliver professional services. This is especially relevant for security system contractors, fire alarm companies, CCTV installers, access control integrators, and low-voltage firms that recommend, design, program, inspect, monitor, or maintain systems.

Examples may include:

  • A client claims the camera system did not capture usable footage.
  • A business alleges the access control system failed after installation.
  • A customer says an alarm system was configured incorrectly.
  • A property owner claims a fire alarm inspection, test, or certification issue caused financial loss.
  • A client alleges the system design did not meet the contract requirements.

Security Alarm E&O Coverage

Some alarm and security contractors may need specialized alarm errors and omissions coverage. This can be especially important for companies involved in burglar alarms, intrusion detection, fire alarm systems, monitoring coordination, video surveillance, or security integration.

A policy review can help determine whether your current insurance includes or excludes claims related to alarm failure, security system failure, monitoring-related allegations, or professional service errors.

Workers’ Compensation Insurance

Low voltage work may involve ladders, lifts, ceiling spaces, attics, basements, warehouses, commercial buildings, wiring, tools, and jobsite hazards. Workers’ compensation can help cover medical expenses and lost wages for covered employees injured in the course of work.

A policy review can help verify employee classification, payroll estimates, officer inclusion or exclusion, subcontractor handling, and audit exposure.

Commercial Auto Insurance

If your business uses vans, trucks, service vehicles, or personal vehicles for company work, commercial auto insurance should be reviewed. Alarm and low voltage contractors often drive between service calls, carry tools and equipment, and visit residential or commercial sites throughout Pennsylvania.

Commercial auto insurance can help protect your business from covered auto liability claims and may be required by contracts, municipalities, or project owners.

Tools and Equipment Insurance

Your tools and equipment are essential to your work. That may include cable testers, meters, ladders, drills, cameras, panels, wire, access control hardware, door strikes, card readers, servers, network equipment, and specialty testing devices.

Tools and equipment coverage, often written as inland marine insurance, can help protect mobile equipment while it is in transit, at a jobsite, or stored temporarily away from your main location.

Installation Floater Coverage

If you are responsible for cameras, panels, wiring, card readers, door hardware, conduit, control boards, sensors, or other materials before installation is complete, installation floater coverage may be important.

This coverage can help protect materials and equipment while they are awaiting installation, in transit, or being installed on a project.

Cyber Liability and Privacy Coverage

Modern alarm, camera, and access control systems may connect to networks, apps, cloud platforms, user credentials, card access logs, video feeds, or customer data. If your work involves network-connected devices, remote access, stored video, customer portals, or system credentials, cyber and privacy coverage should be reviewed.

This may be especially important for security integrators, managed service providers, smart building contractors, camera system contractors, and access control companies.

Umbrella or Excess Liability Insurance

Larger commercial projects may require limits higher than a standard liability policy provides. Umbrella or excess liability coverage can provide additional limits over qualifying underlying policies, such as general liability, commercial auto, and employers’ liability.

This may be important when bidding for work for schools, hospitals, apartment complexes, warehouses, retail chains, government projects, or large general contractors.

Business Owner’s Policy or Commercial Property Insurance

A business owner’s policy may combine general liability, business property, and business interruption coverage for eligible small businesses. The NAIC describes a BOP as a common small business policy that includes general liability, commercial property or business property coverage, and business interruption insurance.

For alarm and low-voltage contractors, this may help protect office property, computers, inventory, business equipment, and certain income interruption exposures.

Contractor Bonds

Some jobs may require a bond before work can begin. Bonds are not the same as insurance, but they may be required for permits, licenses, public jobs, or contract obligations.

FAQ: Alarm & Low Voltage Contractor Insurance in Pennsylvania

Do CCTV installers need insurance in Pennsylvania?

Yes, CCTV installers should carry insurance to protect against claims involving property damage, injury, installation errors, equipment theft, and contract requirements. Commercial clients, general contractors, landlords, and municipalities may require a certificate of insurance before work begins.

What insurance does an alarm system contractor need in Pennsylvania?

Alarm system contractors in Pennsylvania commonly need general liability, professional liability or errors and omissions coverage, workers’ compensation if they have employees, commercial auto, tools and equipment coverage, and umbrella or excess liability. Depending on the work, they may also need cyber liability, installation floater coverage, commercial property, or bonds.

Is access control installation considered low-voltage work?

Access control installation is commonly part of low-voltage contracting because it may involve card readers, door controllers, maglocks, electric strikes, credential systems, intercoms, wiring, and network-connected devices. Insurance should be reviewed to make sure access control work is accurately included in the business description.

Are security systems included under Pennsylvania home improvement rules?

For covered residential work, yes. The Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General’s FAQ lists security systems within the definition of home improvement when the work is connected to certain private residences. The same FAQ says burglar alarm businesses, fire alarm businesses, locksmiths, and similar businesses must register if they perform covered home improvement work.

How much liability insurance does a Pennsylvania home improvement contractor need?

The Pennsylvania Office of Attorney General’s FAQ states that registration requires insurance policy information showing at least $50,000 of personal injury liability coverage and $50,000 of property damage coverage. Higher limits may be required by contracts, municipalities, commercial clients, or general contractors.

Do alarm and low-voltage contractors in Pennsylvania need workers’ compensation?

If the business has employees, workers’ compensation should be reviewed. Pennsylvania states that workers’ compensation insurance is generally mandatory for employers with one or more employees, including part-time or full-time employees and family members.

Does general liability cover failure of an alarm, camera, or access control system?

Not always. General liability is usually focused on third-party bodily injury, property damage, and certain personal or advertising injury claims. Claims involving professional mistakes, design errors, system failure, or failure to perform may require professional liability, errors and omissions, or specialized alarm E&O coverage.

Do low-voltage contractors need cyber liability insurance?

Cyber liability should be considered if the contractor works with network-connected cameras, access control systems, cloud platforms, remote access, customer credentials, video data, or connected security systems. A policy review can help determine whether cyber or privacy-related exposures are covered.

What is a certificate of insurance for an alarm contractor?

A certificate of insurance, or COI, is a document that shows proof of insurance coverage, policy limits, and coverage dates. Alarm, CCTV, access control, and low-voltage contractors often need COIs before starting work for general contractors, commercial clients, landlords, property managers, or municipalities.

Can a general contractor be added as an additional insured?

In many cases, yes. If a contract requires additional insured status, the insurance policy must be reviewed to confirm whether the proper endorsement is available. Some contracts may also require primary and noncontributory wording, waiver of subrogation, or specific completed operations language.
