Why Private Investigation Firms Have Unique Insurance Exposures

Private investigation firms do not face the same risk profile as many other service businesses.

A PI firm may perform surveillance, background investigations, witness interviews, process-related field activity, or investigative support services for attorneys, businesses, insurers, or private clients. That type of work can lead to allegations that go far beyond a standard general liability claim.

What Insurance Do Private Investigators Usually Need?

The right coverage depends on the size of the firm, the services performed, whether employees are on staff, the states involved, and the types of clients being served. Still, there are several coverages that private investigation firms commonly need to review.

General Liability Insurance

General liability insurance helps protect against third-party claims involving bodily injury, property damage, and personal or advertising injury.

For example, if an investigator is accused of causing property damage during an assignment, or a third party alleges injury tied to business operations, general liability may be one of the first coverages reviewed.

Professional Liability / Errors and Omissions Insurance

For many private investigators, this is one of the most important coverages to review.

Professional liability insurance, often called errors and omissions insurance, is intended to address claims arising out of professional services. For PI firms, that can include allegations such as incomplete findings, incorrect reporting, failure to follow instructions, missed details, or work that allegedly caused financial harm to a client.

Workers’ Compensation Insurance

If the business has employees, workers’ compensation may be required by state law.

Private investigators often drive frequently, work in the field, visit unfamiliar locations, and operate in conditions that can create more risk than a standard office-based business. Firms with employees should make sure workers’ compensation requirements and classifications are being reviewed properly.

Commercial Auto or Hired and Non-Owned Auto Coverage

Many private investigation firms rely heavily on vehicles, even if they do not own company cars.

If investigators use personal vehicles for business purposes, hired and non-owned auto coverage may be worth reviewing. If the firm owns vehicles, commercial auto coverage may be needed instead. This becomes especially important when surveillance and travel are central to daily operations.

Umbrella or Excess Liability

Some PI firms work with attorneys, corporations, insurers, or other commercial clients that require higher liability limits.

An umbrella or excess liability policy may help increase available protection above underlying liability policies, depending on the policy structure. It can also help firms meet higher contract requirements.

What Liability Limits Do Private Investigators Need?

Private investigator liability limits should be reviewed based on the type of investigative work being performed, the clients being served, and any contract or certificate of insurance requirements the firm must satisfy.

Many PI firms carry general liability and professional liability coverage, but the right limits are not the same for every business. A solo investigator handling basic background checks may have different needs than a firm performing surveillance, insurance claim investigations, corporate investigations, legal support work, or field assignments involving frequent travel.

  • When reviewing liability limits, private investigators should look at several areas together:
  • General liability limits for bodily injury, property damage, and personal or advertising injury claims.
  • Professional liability or errors and omissions limits for claims tied to investigative work, reporting, missed details, or alleged negligence.
  • Commercial auto or hired and non-owned auto limits if investigators drive for surveillance, interviews, site visits, or client assignments.
  • Umbrella or excess liability limits when contracts, larger clients, attorneys, corporations, or insurers require higher coverage amounts.
  • Certificate requirements that may require specific limits, additional insured wording, waiver of subrogation, or other policy language.

The key question is not only whether the firm has insurance. The more important question is whether the limits and policy wording match the actual work being performed. A private investigation firm may have coverage in place but still fall short of a client contract, attorney requirement, corporate vendor agreement, or claim scenario.

Why Insurance Still Matters for Private Investigation Firms

A common mistake is assuming that insurance needs are straightforward because the business is service-based.

In reality, private investigation work can create layered exposures that involve professional services, field activity, auto use, privacy concerns, and contractual obligations all at once. One assignment, one dispute, or one claim can reveal weaknesses in the policy structure.

That is why insurance for private investigators should be reviewed based on actual operations rather than treated like a generic small-business package.

Common Liability Limit Issues for PI Firms

Some private investigator insurance problems do not show up until a contract, renewal, certificate request, or claim forces a closer review. Common issues include:

  • Professional liability limits that are too low for the type of investigative work being performed.
  • General liability coverage that does not address the firm’s full field exposure.
  • No umbrella or excess liability coverage when a client requires higher limits.
  • Commercial auto or hired and non-owned auto coverage that does not match how investigators actually drive for work.
  • Certificate wording that does not satisfy attorney, corporate, insurer, or government client requirements.
  • Policies that do not clearly reflect surveillance, background investigations, insurance claim investigations, or legal support work.

For private investigators, liability limits should be reviewed before renewal, before signing a larger contract, and before accepting work that creates higher exposure than the firm’s usual assignments.

How Private Investigation Firms Should Review Their Insurance

A useful insurance review usually starts with a few practical questions:

  • What services does the firm actually provide?
  • Does the business perform surveillance or field investigations?
  • Do employees drive for work?
  • Are clients primarily attorneys, businesses, insurers, or private individuals?
  • Are certificates of insurance required?
  • Does the firm handle sensitive digital information?
  • Has the operation changed since the last renewal?

The clearer those details are, the easier it becomes to structure coverage around the actual exposure rather than around assumptions.

FAQ

Do private investigators need professional liability insurance?

In many cases, yes. Private investigators can face claims tied to errors in reporting, missed details, or alleged negligence in professional services, and those exposures are usually different from standard general liability claims.

Is general liability enough for a private investigator?

Usually not by itself. General liability may help with certain third-party bodily injury or property damage claims, but it does not replace professional liability, workers’ compensation, or auto-related coverage where those apply.

Do private investigators need workers’ compensation insurance?

If the firm has employees, workers’ compensation may be required depending on the state. Firms should review this carefully because investigative work often involves driving, site visits, and field-related injury exposure.

What affects the price of private investigator insurance?

Cost can depend on services performed, payroll or revenue, employee count, claims history, vehicle exposure, states of operation, and requested limits. Higher-risk operations or more complex assignments can lead to more underwriting scrutiny.

Do private investigation firms need higher liability limits?

Some private investigators need higher liability limits depending on their contracts, client requirements, services, employee count, vehicle use, and states of operation. A PI firm working with attorneys, insurers, corporations, or government-related contracts may need higher limits than a solo investigator handling smaller assignments.

Private investigators should review general liability, professional liability, commercial auto or hired and non-owned auto, and umbrella or excess liability together. Looking at one policy by itself may not show whether the firm has enough protection for its actual work.

What liability limits should a private investigator carry?

The right liability limits depend on the firm’s services, contracts, client requirements, claims history, vehicle use, and the type of investigations being performed. There is not one limit that fits every private investigation business.

A private investigator should review general liability, professional liability or errors and omissions, commercial auto or hired and non-owned auto, and umbrella or excess liability coverage together. This helps determine whether the firm’s coverage matches its actual work and certificate requirements.
